# What You'll Remember of Your Life

## Pitch
Memory isn't spread evenly across a lifetime. People recall far more from roughly ages 10–30 — the "reminiscence bump" — than from any other stretch. A visualization of a life drawn as a strip of film where frames brighten, blur, and fade by how much survives in memory.

## The Hook
You have already lived through the most memorable years of your life. If you're past 30, the period you'll remember most clearly is behind you — not because nothing matters now, but because of how the brain encodes novelty, identity formation, and emotional intensity.

## Visual Concept
- A horizontal film strip spanning age 0–90 (or to actuarial death)
- Each "frame" is a year, colored/brightened by estimated memory density
- Phases:
  - **0–5**: near total darkness (childhood amnesia)
  - **6–9**: dim, fragmented
  - **10–30**: brightest band — the reminiscence bump
  - **31–50**: moderate, gradual fade
  - **51+**: dimming again, with recency effect near the present
- On scroll: your cursor scrubs along the strip, surfacing what kinds of memories dominate each age window (first loves, identity milestones, cultural touchstones)

## Interactive Layer
- User enters their birth year
- Strip shifts to show *their* bump years mapped to real calendar years (e.g. born 1988 → bump = 1998–2008)
- Could surface what was happening culturally during their bump: music, events, technology

## Data Sources
- **Rubin, Wetzler & Nebes (1986)** — original reminiscence bump paper
- **Conway & Howieson** — autobiographical memory distribution studies
- **Galton (1879)** — earliest cue-word memory distribution work
- **Berntsen & Rubin (2002)** — bump tied to identity formation
- **Rathbone, Moulin & Conway (2008)** — bump persists across cultures
- Meta-analyses of word-cue recall studies (frequency of memories by decade of life)

## Open Questions
- Can we get digitized datasets from published bump studies (memory frequency by age)?
- Is there a self-report angle — ask readers to name their 10 most vivid memories, then show where they cluster?
- Does the bump shift for people who experienced trauma, emigration, or major late-life transitions?

## Tone
Melancholy but not bleak. The bump is not a loss — it's a feature of how meaning gets made. Ends on: the memories you're making right now in your 30s/40s/50s still matter; the recency effect means *today* is always being written.
